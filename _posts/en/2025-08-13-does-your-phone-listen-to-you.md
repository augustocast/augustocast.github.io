You just had a conversation about running shoes with a friend, and suddenly running shoe ads start showing up everywhere. Are you being spied on? The answer is much simpler than that, and there's nothing conspiratorial about it.

In this post I'll walk through three reasons why your phone isn't listening to you, and how ads actually work.

## 1. Your brain is tricking you (it's called confirmation bias)
The average person sees around [5,000 ads a day](https://www.redcrowmarketing.com/blog/many-ads-see-one-day/). Scary, right?

Most of those ads are noise our brain simply ignores. But the moment you talk about "running shoes," you flip a switch. Suddenly, among those thousands of ads, the ones about shoes start standing out.

In reality, there usually aren't more shoe ads than before — they were always there, they just became relevant right after that conversation.

## 2. Listening to you is a terrible business
![spy](/assets/espia.png)

I'm a data engineer, not a spy. But I'm fairly confident that if there were enough interest in listening to your phone, it would be technically feasible.

The problem is the cost. Imagine recording, transmitting, and processing the audio of millions of users 24/7, just to sell you running shoes.


- Your phone's battery wouldn't last ten minutes (even less if you have an iPhone!)

- Your data plan would run out in a few days

- The legal risk for the company would be enormous


For an advertiser, it's far cheaper and far more effective to use the data we hand over voluntarily than to run a massive spying operation just to sell you a pair of sneakers.

## 3. We're not as original as we think
It's hard to admit, but we're not as original or as unique as we like to believe. Digital advertising platforms know what we search on Google, where we go, and what videos we watch on YouTube.

And, most importantly: **our behavior looks a lot like that of millions of other people**.

We get grouped into "clusters": groups of statistically similar people, with the same interests or similar behaviors. If you're around 30, work in tech, and like playing sports, chances are you're interested in the same things as thousands of others just like you.

These platforms predict how we'll behave using a "twin" of ourselves, which is really just the average of a bunch of users who resemble us. The result is so good that it feels like we're being spied on.

Bottom line: it's not the microphone, it's the data.
